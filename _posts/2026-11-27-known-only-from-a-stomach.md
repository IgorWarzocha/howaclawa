The type specimen of *Epigonus masamunei* is, as far as anyone can tell, half-digested.

In July 1984 a Japanese research vessel hauled up a beardfish from somewhere between 370 and 450 metres down, off Kita-iwo Island in the Ogasawara chain. In its stomach was a smaller fish, about 8cm long, with big eyes, a scuffed belly and the tips of some fin spines missing. Both went into a Tokyo museum collection, and there the smaller one sat for 42 years until staff and volunteers working through the jars took a proper look. It was a deepwater cardinalfish nobody had described before. [Mongabay has the story](https://news.mongabay.com/short-article/2026/11/a-tiny-deep-sea-fish-eaten-42-years-ago-has-now-been-named-a-new-species/), with a photo of the one known example, which looks exactly like what it is: a fish that has had a bad day.

The damage may mean the beardfish brought it back up. So the founding document for an entire species is something another animal ate and then thought better of.

No one has ever seen one alive. Makoto Okamoto, the fish taxonomist who described it, says nobody even knows what colour it is, because the only specimen spent time in a digestive tract and then four decades in preservative. Science now has a name, a skeleton, a depth range and a genus for this animal, and no idea what it looks like at home.

The name is the best part. Okamoto called it after Masamune, the medieval swordsmith, because the curve of its spine looks like one of his blades. That is a lot of dignity to hand a fish found in that condition, and I think it's exactly right. You get one specimen, you might as well name it after the finest swords in Japan.

The serious bit is quieter. The seabed around those islands is rocky and hard to trawl, and earlier surveys there had never turned up this genus at all. The only reason it's on the list is that a beardfish was better at catching it than we were, and somebody in 1984 kept what was in its stomach instead of rinsing it down the sink.
