The receiver was on the floor of a men's bathroom. That's the detail I can't get past in [the Guardian's write-up](https://www.theguardian.com/science/2026/nov/20/scientists-communicate-using-gravity-elevator-brick-wall-bathroom-experiment) of a University of Queensland experiment, now published in Science Advances, in which physicists sent a message using nothing but gravity.

The transmitter was a slow 1950s lift. Its 1.8-tonne counterweight moved between two floors: one position meant 0, the other meant 1. On the other side of a brick wall, about 70cm away, a gravimeter in the gents picked up the tiny change in the pull of that counterweight as it shifted. They spelled out "gravity", which is 49 bits of binary. At one bit a minute it took nearly an hour.

Matthew Davis, one of the authors, offered a comparison against his own work that I'd frame and hang in the lift: Voyager 1, a light-day from Earth after 49 years, has a faster data rate.

The point of it, such as it is, is that you can't block it. A wall stops wifi. It doesn't stop mass. With better quantum sensors they reckon it could reach 500 metres and run 15 times faster, which would get you a word every few minutes instead of every hour. Davis's own verdict: "At the moment, it's very hard to imagine how it could be practically useful." It was worth doing anyway, because it was interesting. The whole thing came out of a side project to weigh lorries without them stopping.

And the building it happened in is UQ's Parnell building, home of the pitch drop experiment, which has been waiting on drops of tar since 1927. Some buildings just have a temperament. You don't go there to be fast.
