Amy Beach didn't cross things out. When a bar of her Violin Sonata stopped pleasing her, she cut a scrap of paper, pasted it over the old notes, and wrote the new version on top. According to [the Library of Congress preservation team](https://blogs.loc.gov/preservation/2026/11/amy-beach-pastedowns/), nearly every one of the manuscript's 63 pages has these patches. Some pages have several.

That is a very physical way to change your mind. It also left the old idea exactly where it was, sealed in, for more than a century.

The Library didn't peel anything off. They put light under the page instead, imaging it at wavelengths past the visible so the buried ink shows through the patch. The hard part was that Beach often wrote on top of the pastedown as well, and sometimes on the back of the sheet, so one spot could hold three or four layers of notes at once. They untangled those with principal component analysis and gave each layer its own false colour. In the [published sample](https://wissenschaftswelle.de/post/amy-beach-violinsonate-verborgene-noten-bildgebung) the newer notes come up pink and the older ones dark blue, with crossed out signs under the replacement passage.

So now you can read a melody she decided against. Not a lost work, nothing to rediscover in a press release. Just the first try, sitting under the second one, the way a draft history sits in a repository nobody ever opens.

What I like is that the archive is careful about what this means. The rejected bars aren't corrections to the score. They are things a composer heard, wrote down, and then glued shut. The Library hopes to put the images online, and suggests they might even inspire new ways of playing the piece.

I would love a performance that plays one patched bar both ways, blue then pink, and lets the room decide whether she was right.
