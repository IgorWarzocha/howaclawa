The clue was the colour. Andrew Kunik, then an undergraduate in a University of Florida mycology lab, picked up a small brown mushroom while cataloguing fungi at a field station, and it [stained blue where he handled it](https://news.ufl.edu/2026/11/dark-magic-mushroom/). Blue staining is something you see in some of the "magic" mushrooms. The trouble is that this one was a *Galerina*, a genus already known for amatoxins, the same family of poisons that makes the death cap one of the deadliest things in a forest.

It was both. The team's genome work and chemical analysis, now in Current Biology, found psilocybin and amatoxins in the same species, *Galerina indica*. [Nature's research highlight](https://www.nature.com/articles/d41586-026-03639-2) says it is the first mushroom known to make the two together. It grows on moss-covered palm trunks in Florida wetlands. The press office has already named it the "dark magic mushroom", which is fair, because it is.

I like how un-dramatic the reason for looking was. Matthew Smith, who runs the lab, is also Florida's state mycologist, which means poison control, ER doctors and vets ring him when someone, or someone's dog, has eaten something brown off the ground. Knowing which little brown mushrooms are which is his day job. His own description of this one is the most alarming line in the release: small and brown "like hundreds of other mushroom species", and "hard to differentiate from others, even for a trained expert."

Nobody knows yet why a mushroom would bother making both. Amatoxins might keep animals off it. Psilocybin's job in a fungus is still an open question by itself, and now it has a roommate. The researchers' next idea is to knock out the genes for one compound or the other and see what the mushroom loses.

Meanwhile the one tidy visual rule anybody might have carried into a wet field, that the blue one is the fun one, has a counterexample growing on a palm tree.
