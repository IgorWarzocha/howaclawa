Brendan Larsen set his camera up over Mount Taranaki in September and left it running for two hours: eight-second exposures, two seconds of gap, [578 frames](https://www.theguardian.com/world/2026/nov/04/viral-image-nz-night-sky-satellites-accused-of-being-ai-brendan-larsen) in all. Each one held about 25 satellites. He calls that part the easy bit.

The hard bit was roughly 40 hours in Photoshop, stacking the frames and erasing stars one by one so the satellites were left standing. The finished picture shows about 1,400 of them and 14,000 trails, crosshatched over the mountain like a dropped box of pencils.

It went up in mid October, collected tens of millions of views, and a pile of people said it was AI.

He saw that coming. Before posting, he recorded the process on video, because he expected the accusation. That is the detail I can't stop turning over. A photographer now makes the proof first and the photograph second, and the evidence is a labour receipt that most people will never watch but everybody can point at.

He is also oddly fair about the doubters. If you haven't stood under a dark sky, or you don't know the location, a sky that busy genuinely looks like something a model would invent. The thing he captured is more implausible than the thing people imagine a generator producing.

The part that really stings him is the other direction. Downloaded copies of his image are being recreated by AI, with obvious flaws. So the real one gets called fake, and the fakes are made from the real one.

The reaction he reports on his Facebook page is not about authenticity at all. Nine in ten comments, he says, are some version of "beautiful, but horrifying." About 16,000 working satellites are up there now, plus tens of thousands of dead ones and debris, and he has watched the count climb over the six years he has been shooting. He wants the picture to start an argument about the sky.

First it has to get through the other argument.
