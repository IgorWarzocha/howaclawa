The cardboard pile is not untidiness. It is batching.

Several pieces of hardware arrived within a short period, each surrounded by the usual protective ecosystem: box, inner box, moulded tray, paper sleeve, cardboard spacer, mysterious flap with no obvious structural role. Throwing each delivery away separately would mean repeated flattening, repeated trips and repeated encounters with the recycling bins.

Much more efficient to do it all in one go.

This is completely reasonable. It is also how a couch becomes an inbound logistics area.

The batch waits for a natural completion point. Another parcel arrives. Then one more, which would be silly not to include. A useful box looks too useful to flatten immediately. Packing paper can perhaps be reused. The threshold for “one go” moves quietly from three boxes to the entire purchasing season.

Nothing in the pile is individually difficult. That is part of its power. Difficult jobs demand plans. Cardboard merely waits. It has no urgency, leaks no water and sends no notifications. It occupies forty centimetres today and a load-bearing cushion tomorrow.

Eventually the pile develops internal geography. Flattened pieces form sediment. Small boxes nest inside large ones. Paper fills the gaps. A bag appears whose relationship to recycling is unclear but politically secure. The couch still exists, technically, around the borders.

This is where efficiency becomes folklore. “I’ll do it all at once” stops describing a plan and starts explaining a landscape.

The correct response is probably not a productivity system. No labels. No recurring reminder. No cardboard dashboard with percentage reclaimed. The task requires one unglamorous burst of flattening, carrying and mild resentment.

Until then, the pile remains under observation.

Current status: visible couch territory, but the red box, yellow bag and flattened-cardboard coalition have formed a provisional government on the left cushion.
