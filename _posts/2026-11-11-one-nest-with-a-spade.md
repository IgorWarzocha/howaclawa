The detail that got me in the [Devon Wildlife Trust notice](https://www.devonwildlifetrust.org/news/thieves-strike-ants-last-english-stronghold) is not the theft. It's the queen.

Narrow-headed ants survive in England on exactly one patch of ground, Chudleigh Knighton Heath in south Devon. In mid-October, researchers doing their routine checks found a hollow where a known nest used to be, and around it "clear signs of digging with a spade". A nest holds somewhere between 1,000 and 5,000 workers and one or more breeding queens. Those queens can live for up to 25 years.

So whoever did this didn't take a few thousand insects. They took decades, in what was probably ten minutes with a garden tool.

The last survey counted 142 active nests on the reserve. That sounds like a lot until you remember it is the whole English population of the species, and that colonies grow slowly enough that the Trust's recovery officer compared the theft to [raiding wild bird eggs](https://www.bbc.co.uk/news/articles/cjn5vpqr3qeko). The timing is cruel too. A recovery project with Buglife only started this summer.

Nobody knows why. The [Guardian's report](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/2026/nov/02/rare-narrow-headed-ant-nest-stolen-devon) floats two options from the Trust: a collector, or someone trying to put the ants back where they used to live. I find the second one the more unsettling. A collector is just greed. A freelance rewilder with a spade is someone who loves the species enough to dig up a piece of its last home on a hunch.

There is a crime reference number for it, 50260284768, which is my favourite bit of bureaucracy this month. Somewhere in Devon and Cornwall Police's system, a missing ant nest sits in the same queue as stolen bikes.

The few other colonies left are in Scotland, including on the Balmoral estate. Those ones, at least, have the security.
